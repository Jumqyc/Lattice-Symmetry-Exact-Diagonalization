\title{Symmetry-resolved exact diagonalization of lattice models}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Exact diagonalization is limited by the size of the Hilbert space, and the
symmetries of a lattice Hamiltonian are what make that space smaller. This note
works out, in a form that can be implemented directly, the decomposition of the
Hilbert space of a finite lattice model under its space group: translation
sectors labelled by momentum, the little group at each momentum, the induced
representations that complete the classification, and the isotypic projectors
that produce an orthonormal basis in which the Hamiltonian is block diagonal.
The treatment allows several orbits per unit cell, an action on the local
orbitals that is a permutation up to phases, non-symmorphic elements, and the
internal spin flip. The cost of each step is stated, and the numerical checks
that pin the construction down are collected at the end.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The Hilbert space of a lattice model with $L$ sites and local dimension $d$ has
dimension $d^L$, and exact diagonalization needs the matrix elements of the
Hamiltonian between all of them. Nothing about the Schr\"odinger equation
requires that much work: a symmetry of the Hamiltonian splits the space into
independent blocks, and a block of dimension $M$ costs $M^3$ instead of $d^{3L}$.
The purpose of this note is to describe that splitting completely, for the
symmetry group that a finite lattice actually has, in a form that is neither
restricted to the cubic lattices of the textbooks nor to the symmorphic
subgroup.

The group in question is the space group of the finite lattice: translations by
supercell vectors, rotations and reflections that preserve the supercell, and
the combinations of a rotation with a fractional translation that a glide plane
or a screw axis provides. It acts on the computational basis by permuting the
sites and, at every site, by a monomial map on the local states; the latter is
what allows a unit cell with several orbitals and a nontrivial action on them.
An internal symmetry, the spin flip, is independent of all this and is treated
separately.

The decomposition proceeds in two steps, and the two steps are the reason the
whole thing is practical. Translations form an abelian normal subgroup, so their
irreducible representations are one-dimensional and labelled by momentum; the
projector onto a momentum sector is a short sum of $|G_{\mathrm{tr}}|$ elements.
What is left of the point group at that momentum is the little group, and inside
a momentum sector its projector is also short. The representation theory involved
is the classical one\cite{fulton1991representation,serre1977}. The alternative, projecting once
with the idempotent of the full space-group irrep, requires summing over all
$|G_{\mathrm{latt}}|$ elements for every sector; the two-step route replaces this by
$|G_{\mathrm{tr}}| + |G_{\mathbf{n}}|$ and, more importantly, keeps every
intermediate object small.

Sections~\ref{sec:setting} and~\ref{sec:actions} fix the conventions and the
action of the group on the computational basis. Sections~\ref{sec:momentum}
and~\ref{sec:little} develop the two steps, together with the induced
representations that account for the sectors of one momentum star
(Section~\ref{sec:induced}). Section~\ref{sec:blocks} turns the projectors into
blocks, Section~\ref{sec:spin} adds the spin flip, and
Sections~\ref{sec:algorithm} and~\ref{sec:checks} state the algorithm with its
cost and list the numerical evidence that it is correct.
Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls} collects the mistakes that this construction invites.

Throughout, $d$ is the spatial dimension (mostly $d=2$), and $L$ is the number of
sites of the finite lattice, which is not the number of unit cells when a cell
carries several orbits.

\section{Setting and notation}\label{sec:setting}

\subsection{The lattice}

A unit cell is spanned by the vectors $\mathbf{a}_1,\dots,\mathbf{a}_d$ and
contains a finite set of sites, each specified by its position $\mathbf{p}$ in the
cell and by a \emph{species} $s$. The species is the equivalence class that a
symmetry must preserve: two sites of the same species may be exchanged by a
symmetry, two sites of different species may not. This one bit of bookkeeping
covers both the sublattices of a honeycomb and the inequivalent sites of an
arbitrary multi-site cell, and it is the only place where the geometry of the
cell enters the group theory.

The finite lattice is obtained by repeating the cell $N_i$ times along
$\mathbf{a}_i$ and imposing periodic boundary conditions. Its supercell vectors
are
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{A}_i = N_i\,\mathbf{a}_i, \qquad i = 1,\dots,d,
\end{equation}
it contains $L = n_{\mathrm{cell}}\prod_i N_i$ sites, and a site is specified by
its position in the cell together with the integer vector $\mathbf{c} \in
\mathbb{Z}^d$ of the cell that contains it. Sites are numbered
$j = 0,\dots,L-1$; the numbering is arbitrary but fixed once and for all, since
it is the numbering that the basis vectors of the computer representation carry.

\subsection{The local space and the computational basis}

Each site carries a local space of dimension $d_{\mathrm{loc}}$, with an
orthonormal basis $\ket{\sigma}$ indexed by $\sigma = 0,\dots,d_{\mathrm{loc}}-1$.
The computational basis of the full Hilbert space consists of the product states
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\sigma_1\dots\sigma_L}, \qquad \sigma_j \in \{0,\dots,d_{\mathrm{loc}}-1\},
\end{equation}
and $\Hil$ is their span, of dimension $d_{\mathrm{loc}}^L$. A local charge
$q(\sigma)$ is used to select a sector: the subspace with total charge $Q$ is
spanned by the states with
\begin{equation}
  Q(\sigma_1\dots\sigma_L) = \sum_{j=1}^{L} q(\sigma_j) = Q .
  \label{eq:charge}
\end{equation}
The charge is conserved by the models considered here (it is the magnetisation
for a spin model), and fixing it reduces the dimension from $d_{\mathrm{loc}}^L$ to
the coefficient of $x^Q$ in $\bigl(\sum_\sigma x^{q(\sigma)}\bigr)^L$.

\subsection{The Hamiltonian}

The Hamiltonian is a sum of local terms,
\begin{equation}
  H = \sum_{a} J_a\, O_a, \qquad
  O_a = o^{(a)}_{j_1}\otimes \dots \otimes o^{(a)}_{j_r},
  \label{eq:hamiltonian}
\end{equation}
where each $o^{(a)}_{j}$ acts on the local space of site $j$ and is the identity
elsewhere, $r$ is small (two, for a two-body interaction), and $J_a$ is real for
a Hermitian $H$. A term that acts on \emph{different} sites may have different
local operators on each of them, which is how an anisotropic or a
symmetry-broken coupling is written; a term that acts on the same pair of sites
in several ways is simply listed several times.

\section{The action of the group on the basis}\label{sec:actions}

\subsection{Elements of the space group}

An element of the space group is an affine map of the plane,
\begin{equation}
  g = (\mathbf{t}, R): \quad \mathbf{x}\mapsto R\mathbf{x} + \mathbf{t},
  \label{eq:affine}
\end{equation}
with $R$ orthogonal and $\mathbf{t}$ a translation. The translation part need not
be a lattice vector: a glide plane is a reflection with
$\mathbf{t}$ equal to half a lattice vector, and a screw axis is a rotation with
a fractional translation along the axis. Such elements are included from the
start. Composition is
\begin{equation}
  (\mathbf{t}_1,R_1)(\mathbf{t}_2,R_2) = (\mathbf{t}_1 + R_1\mathbf{t}_2,\,
  R_1R_2),
  \qquad
  (\mathbf{t},R)^{-1} = (-R^{-1}\mathbf{t},\,R^{-1}),
  \label{eq:composition}
\end{equation}
so that the map on the left is applied last.

Pure translations form the abelian subgroup $G_{\mathrm{tr}} \cong \mathbb{Z}_{N_1}
\times\cdots\times \mathbb{Z}_{N_d}$, and the rotations that appear in
$G_{\mathrm{latt}}$ form the point group $\Gpt$. For a symmorphic group,
$\Glatt = \Gtr \rtimes \Gpt$; for a non-symmorphic one the group is not a semidirect
product, but every element is still a pair $(\mathbf{t},R)$ and the composition
law \eqref{eq:composition} is unchanged. Nothing below needs the semidirect
structure except when we count elements, and there the counts are by
construction.

\subsection{Which elements are symmetries of the finite lattice}

The infinite lattice is invariant under a continuum of translations, but the
finite torus is not, and two conditions decide whether a given element survives.

First, the rotation must preserve the supercell lattice, since otherwise it maps
the torus onto a different torus. It does so exactly when it maps every supercell
vector to an integer combination of them,
\begin{equation}
  R\,\mathbf{A}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{d} M_{ji}\,\mathbf{A}_j,
  \label{eq:intmat}
\end{equation}
with $M$ an integer matrix of determinant $\pm 1$. The matrix $M$ is computed once
per operation and is the only information about the rotation that the momentum
bookkeeping requires.

Second, the affine map must permute the sites. For every site $j$ there must be
exactly one site $\pi_g(j)$ of the same species with
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{x}_{\pi_g(j)} \equiv R\,\mathbf{x}_j + \mathbf{t}
  \pmod{\text{supercell lattice}},
  \label{eq:sitemap}
\end{equation}
and the map $j \mapsto \pi_g(j)$ must be a bijection. A position search that only
looks in a neighbourhood of the cell fails here: a legitimate operation may send
a site to a periodic image far outside the cell that was searched. Comparing
fractional coordinates modulo the supercell, on the other hand, is exact, and it
is what makes multi-site cells with several orbits work.

The set of elements that pass both tests is closed under composition and
inversion, so it forms a group; it is this group, of order
$|\Glatt| = |\Gtr|\,|\Gpt|$, that is used below. Note that the point group of the
finite torus can be smaller than that of the infinite lattice: a fourfold rotation
is a symmetry of the square lattice but not of a $2\times4$ torus, whose aspect
ratio it would change. The first test above detects this without any case
analysis.

\subsection{The action on the computational basis}

A space-group element permutes the sites, and at each site it acts on the local
state. The two combine into
\begin{equation}
  U(g)\ket{\sigma_1\dots\sigma_L} = \varphi_g(\sigma)\,
  \ket{\sigma'_1\dots\sigma'_L},
  \qquad
  \sigma'_j = \rho_{g,j}\bigl(\sigma_{\pi_g^{-1}(j)}\bigr) ,
  \label{eq:action}
\end{equation}
where $\rho_{g,j}$ is a local \emph{monomial} map: it sends one local basis state
to one local basis state times a phase, and $\varphi_g(\sigma)$ is the product of
those phases. The action is therefore a permutation of the computational basis up
to phases, and it is a genuine representation, $U(g)U(h) = U(gh)$.

The monomial requirement is a real restriction on the local basis, and it is worth
seeing what it excludes. Take a single site with two $p$ orbitals and a
fourfold rotation. In the Cartesian basis $(p_x,p_y)$ the rotation acts as the
real matrix $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, and a basis state
$\ket{p_x}$ is sent to a superposition of the two orbitals; the image of a
product state is then a sum of product states, and the ``basis'' of adapted
vectors is no longer indexed by single orbit representatives. In the circular
basis $p_\pm = (p_x\pm \ii p_y)/\sqrt2$ the same rotation is diagonal,
$R\ket{p_\pm} = \ee^{\mp\ii\theta}\ket{p_\pm}$, and a reflection exchanges the
two. In this basis the action is monomial, and the whole construction goes
through. The general rule is that one works in the local basis that diagonalizes
the abelian part of the local representation and permutes the rest, which for
two-dimensional orbitals is the basis of circular harmonics
$(x\pm\ii y)^m$: a rotation multiplies them by a phase and a reflection exchanges
$m$ with $-m$\cite{tinkham1964}.

Two elements of the group are special enough to name. The identity acts
trivially. A translation by the integer vector $\mathbf{c}$ acts as
\begin{equation}
  U(\mathbf{c})\ket{\sigma_1\dots\sigma_L} = \ket{\sigma'_1\dots\sigma'_L},
  \qquad
  \sigma'_{j} = \sigma_{\pi_{\mathbf{c}}^{-1}(j)},
  \label{eq:translation}
\end{equation}
with no phase, since a translation does not touch the local states.

\section{Translation sectors}\label{sec:momentum}

\subsection{Characters and momentum labels}

Because $G_{\mathrm{tr}}$ is abelian, its irreducible representations are
one-dimensional and labelled by an integer vector $\mathbf{n} = (n_1,\dots,n_d)$
with $n_i = 0,\dots,N_i-1$:
\begin{equation}
  \chi_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{c}) = \exp\biggl(2\pi \ii \sum_{i=1}^{d}
  \frac{n_i c_i}{N_i}\biggr).
  \label{eq:char}
\end{equation}
The physical momentum is $\mathbf{k} = \sum_i (n_i/N_i)\mathbf{b}_i$, with
$\mathbf{b}_i$ the reciprocal vectors of the unit cell, so that
$\chi_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{c}) = \ee^{\ii\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{c}}}$;
the label $\mathbf{n}$ is the more convenient variable, because it is an integer
and every comparison below is a comparison modulo $N_i$.

\subsection{The projector and the sector}

The projector onto momentum $\mathbf{n}$ is
\begin{equation}
  P_{\mathbf{n}} = \frac{1}{|\Gtr|}\sum_{\mathbf{c} \in \Gtr}
  \chi_{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(\mathbf{c})\, U(\mathbf{c}),
  \label{eq:Pn}
\end{equation}
and $P_{\mathbf{n}}P_{\mathbf{n}'} = \delta_{\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}'}P_{\mathbf{n}}$,
$\sum_{\mathbf{n}}P_{\mathbf{n}} = \mathbb{1}$. For a Hamiltonian that commutes
with translations, $[H,P_{\mathbf{n}}] = 0$, and the Hilbert space splits as
\begin{equation}
  \Hil = \bigoplus_{\mathbf{n}} \Hil_{\mathbf{n}}, \qquad
  \Hil_{\mathbf{n}} = P_{\mathbf{n}}\Hil .
\end{equation}
This is Bloch's theorem for a finite torus, stated as a projector; the
momentum sector is the standard starting point of symmetry-resolved exact
diagonalization\cite{sandvik2010}.

\subsection{An orthonormal basis of a momentum sector}

Translations act on the computational basis by permutation, so they partition it
into orbits. Let $\mathcal{O}(x)$ be the orbit of the basis state $\ket{x}$,
$x$ its chosen representative (the smallest state, say), and
$\Stab(x) = \{\mathbf{c} : U(\mathbf{c})\ket{x} = \ket{x}\}$ its stabilizer in
$G_{\mathrm{tr}}$. The state
\begin{equation}
  \ket{\mathbf{n};x} = \frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{n}}(x)}\,P_{\mathbf{n}}\ket{x}
  \label{eq:adapt}
\end{equation}
belongs to $\Hil_{\mathbf{n}}$ whenever its norm does not vanish, and
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{n}}(x)\bigr)^2 = \mel{x}{P_{\mathbf{n}}}{x}
  = \frac{1}{|\Gtr|}\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\Stab(x)}
  \chi_{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(\mathbf{c}).
  \label{eq:norm}
\end{equation}
For a generic momentum the stabilizer is trivial, the norm is
$|\Gtr|^{-1/2}$, and nothing is lost. At a symmetric momentum the sum in
\eqref{eq:norm} can vanish; the orbit then contributes nothing to that sector,
and \eqref{eq:adapt} is discarded. This is not an accident to be patched: a
momentum sector that no state can carry is simply empty, and the counting below
accounts for it automatically.

The vectors \eqref{eq:adapt} are orthonormal. Two states in different orbits are
related by no translation, so their projectors have disjoint support in the
computational basis; two states in the same orbit have been identified, and the
remaining factor is the normalization. The collection over all orbits with
$\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{n}}(x)\neq 0$ is therefore an orthonormal basis of
$\Hil_{\mathbf{n}}$, of dimension $M_{\mathbf{n}}$ equal to the number of such
orbits, and
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\mathbf{n}} M_{\mathbf{n}} = d_{\mathrm{loc}}^{L} .
\end{equation}

\subsection{The Hamiltonian in a momentum sector}

Writing the basis \eqref{eq:adapt} as the columns of a matrix $B_{\mathbf{n}}$,
the block is
\begin{equation}
  \bigl(H_{\mathbf{n}}\bigr)_{xx'} = \mel{\mathbf{n};x}{H}{\mathbf{n};x'}
  = \frac{1}{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{n}}(x)\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{n}}(x')}
  \frac{1}{|\Gtr|}\sum_{\mathbf{c}}
  \chi_{\mathbf{n}}^{*}(\mathbf{c})\,\mel{x}{H\,U(\mathbf{c})}{x'},
  \label{eq:Hn}
\end{equation}
where the step uses $[H,U(\mathbf{c})] = 0$ and reduces the double sum over
translations to a single one. In practice one does not evaluate
\eqref{eq:Hn} element by element: $B_{\mathbf{n}}$ is sparse, with at most
$|\Gtr|$ entries per column, and $H_{\mathbf{n}} = B_{\mathbf{n}}^{\dagger} H
B_{\mathbf{n}}$ is formed as a sparse product, whose cost is that of the
nonzeros of the intermediate $HB_{\mathbf{n}}$.

\section{The point group at a fixed momentum}\label{sec:little}

\subsection{How a rotation acts on the label}

Let $R$ be a point-group operation and $M$ the integer matrix of
\eqref{eq:intmat}. A translation by $\mathbf{c}$ moves a position
$\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{x} + \sum_i c_i\mathbf{A}_i$; conjugating it by $R$ gives
the translation by the rotated vector,
\begin{equation}
  R\Bigl(\sum_i c_i\mathbf{A}_i\Bigr) = \sum_j (M\mathbf{c})_j\,\mathbf{A}_j,
  \qquad (M\mathbf{c})_j = \sum_i M_{ji}c_i ,
  \label{eq:Mpos}
\end{equation}
so translation labels transform as $\mathbf{c}\mapsto M\mathbf{c}$. Momentum
labels are dual to them: the character must be invariant,
$\chi_{\mathbf{n}}(M\mathbf{c}) = \chi_{M^{T}\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{c})$, hence
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{n} \mapsto M^{T}\mathbf{n}, \qquad
  (M^{T}\mathbf{n})_i = \sum_j M_{ji}\,n_j .
  \label{eq:Mmom}
\end{equation}

\subsection{The little group, and the trap that it hides}

The little group of $\mathbf{n}$ is the subgroup of the point group that leaves
the label invariant,
\begin{equation}
  \Gk = \{\,R \in \Gpt : M^{T}(R)\,\mathbf{n} \equiv \mathbf{n}
  \pmod{N_i}\ \text{for every } i\,\}.
  \label{eq:little}
\end{equation}
The congruence is taken component by component modulo $N_i$, and it is essential
that it is taken that way. Because the labels live in
$\mathbb{Z}_{N_1}\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}_{N_d}$, one is tempted to state the
condition as ``$R\mathbf{k}$ equals $\mathbf{k}$ up to a reciprocal lattice
vector''. On a finite torus that test is empty: the allowed momenta are already
integer combinations of the \emph{supercell} reciprocal vectors
$\mathbf{b}_i/N_i$, so every allowed momentum differs from every other by a
supercell reciprocal lattice vector, and the condition is satisfied by every
operation at every momentum. The little group then comes out equal to the whole
point group, the point-group step of the decomposition does nothing, and the
sector labels it produces are not labels of anything.

The size of the effect is easy to state. For the $4\times4$ square lattice with
$D_4$ there are $16$ momenta and $8$ operations, so $128$ pairs $(\mathbf{n},R)$;
the correct test selects $2\cdot 8 + 2\cdot 4 + 12\cdot 2 = 48$ of them, with
little groups of order $8$ at $\Gamma$ and at $(2,2)$, of order $4$ at $(2,0)$
and $(0,2)$, and of order $2$ at the remaining twelve labels, while the vacuous
test selects all $128$.

\subsection{The action inside a sector, and the Bloch phase}

Assume now that $R \in \Gk$. Since $U(R)$ commutes with $P_{\mathbf{n}}$ up to the
relabelling \eqref{eq:Mmom}, and $R$ fixes the label, $U(R)$ maps
$\Hil_{\mathbf{n}}$ onto itself. On the adapted basis it acts as a \emph{phased}
permutation:
\begin{equation}
  U(R)\ket{\mathbf{n};x} = \omega_{R}(x)\,\ket{\mathbf{n};x'}, \qquad
  |\omega_R(x)| = 1 .
  \label{eq:phased}
\end{equation}
The phase is not a convention of the diagonalization; it is fixed by the
definition \eqref{eq:adapt} and can be read off. If $U(R)\ket{x} =
\varphi\ket{x_1}$ and $\ket{x_1} = U(\mathbf{c}_0)\ket{x'}$ relates the image
state to the representative of its orbit, then
\begin{equation}
  \omega_R(x) = \varphi\,\chi_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{c}_0)\,
  \frac{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{n}}(x')}{\mathcal{N}_{\mathbf{n}}(x)} ,
\end{equation}
because $P_{\mathbf{n}}$ acting on a translated state multiplies by the Bloch
phase $\chi_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{c}_0)$. Keeping this phase is a matter of
correctness rather than elegance: with unit phases in its place the sector
Hamiltonians are not the restriction of $H$, and the spectra are wrong by
amounts of order the bandwidth: on the $3\times3$ square lattice with $D_4$,
dropping the phase moved the spectrum by $1.8$.

Because $U(R)$ is a permutation up to phases, the sector carries a genuine
representation of $\Gk$ on $\mathbb{C}^{M_{\mathbf{n}}}$: each element is a
monomial matrix, and no dense matrix is needed to store it.

\subsection{Non-symmorphic elements}

The criterion \eqref{eq:little} uses only $R$, not the translation part of $g$.
This is not an oversight. The fractional translation of a glide or a screw acts
on positions and therefore on the labels only through $R$; on states it
contributes a phase that is already contained in the monomial action
\eqref{eq:action} and, inside a sector, in the phase \eqref{eq:phased}. So the
label bookkeeping is exactly as for the symmorphic case, and the non-symmorphic
character of the element reappears where it belongs, in the phases.

\subsection{Induced representations}\label{sec:induced}

The sectors labelled by one momentum are not the whole story: the irreducible
representations of the space group collect the whole \emph{star} of momenta
$\{\mathbf{n}'\}$ related to $\mathbf{n}$ by the point group. The classification
is Mackey's induction\cite{serre1977,bc1972}, and it is worth recording because it
is what makes the bookkeeping of dimensions consistent.

Let $H_{\mathbf{n}}$ be the subgroup of $\Glatt$ of elements whose rotation lies in
$\Gk$; its elements are the pairs $(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{c}}, R)$ with
$R\in\Gk$. Its irreducible representations are labelled by $\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
  \rho_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{c}}, R) =
  \chi_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{c})\,\chi_{\alpha}(R).
  \label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
This is a representation precisely because of the little-group condition: for it
to be a homomorphism one needs
$\chi_{\mathbf{n}}(\mathbf{c}) = \chi_{\mathbf{n}}(M(R)\mathbf{c})$, that is
$M(R)^{T}\mathbf{n}\equiv\mathbf{n}$, which is \eqref{eq:little}. The
irreducibility condition and the validity of the induced representation are
therefore the same equation.

Let $S$ be a set of left coset representatives of $\Gk$ in $\Gpt$ and write
$s = (0,R_s)$. The induced character is, by definition,
\begin{equation}
  \chi^{\Glatt}_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}(g) = \sum_{s\in S}
  \widetilde{\chi}_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}(s^{-1}gs),
  \qquad
  \widetilde{\chi}(h) =
  \begin{cases}
    \chi(h), & h \in H_{\mathbf{n}},\\
    0, & \text{otherwise},
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}
and evaluating $s^{-1}(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{c}},R)s$ with \eqref{eq:composition}
gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{\;
  \chi^{\Glatt}_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}(\mathbf{t}_{\mathbf{c}}, R)
  = \sum_{s\in S}
  \chi_{\mathbf{n}}\bigl(M(R_s)^{-1}\mathbf{c}\bigr)\;
  \widetilde{\chi}_{\alpha}\bigl(R_s^{-1} R R_s\bigr)
  \;}
  \label{eq:indchar}
\end{equation}
with $\widetilde{\chi}_\alpha$ extended by zero outside $\Gk$. Its value at the
identity is the dimension,
\begin{equation}
  d_{\mathbf{n},\alpha} = \frac{|\Gpt|}{|\Gk|}\,d_{\alpha} = |\Star(\mathbf{n})|\,d_{\alpha},
  \label{eq:dim}
\end{equation}
and the induced characters are orthonormal,
$\langle\chi_{\mathbf{n},\alpha},\chi_{\mathbf{n}',\alpha'}\rangle_{\Glatt} =
\delta_{\mathbf{n}\mathbf{n}'}\delta_{\alpha\alpha'}$ with the inner product normalized by $|\Glatt|$, provided the sum
runs over one momentum per star. That restriction is not cosmetic: summing over
every momentum in the Brillouin zone instead counts each irreducible
representation once per member of its star. For the $4\times4$ square lattice
with $D_4$ the unrestricted sum of $d^2$ gives $432$ where the group has order
$128$.

Two remarks connect \eqref{eq:indchar} with the computation.

First, the sectors produced in Section~\ref{sec:momentum} and
Section~\ref{sec:little} are not the irreducible representations
\eqref{eq:indchar}; they are the pieces into which one irreducible
representation splits when it is restricted to the translations. An induced
representation of dimension $|\Star(\mathbf{n})|d_\alpha$ contributes a sector
of dimension $m_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}d_\alpha$ at each momentum of its star, with
the same multiplicity $m_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}$ for all of them, and the sum of
$d_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}^2$ over the star is the sum of the sector dimensions.
The decomposition of the Hilbert space is complete either way; the sectors are
what one diagonalizes, and the induced representations are what one counts
with.

Second, the formula \eqref{eq:indchar} is often written with the inverses
elsewhere, summing over a \emph{right} transversal with $R_s R R_s^{-1}$ and
$\mathbf{c}$ rather than $M^{-1}\mathbf{c}$. The two forms differ by the
relabelling $s\mapsto s^{-1}$ and are the same sum. What matters in either form
is that $S$ is a transversal: one representative per coset, no repeats. A set of
operations that merely avoids the little group is not a transversal, and the
resulting sum is neither a character nor an integer.

\section{Isotypic blocks}\label{sec:blocks}

\subsection{The projector onto an irrep}

Within the momentum sector the little group acts by phased permutations
\eqref{eq:phased}, so the projector onto the $\alpha$-isotypic component is the
familiar average
\begin{equation}
  \Pi_{\alpha} = \frac{d_\alpha}{|\Gk|}\sum_{R\in\Gk}
  \chi_\alpha^{*}(R)\,U(R).
  \label{eq:Pi}
\end{equation}
It is Hermitian and idempotent, $\Pi_\alpha^2 = \Pi_\alpha$ and
$\Pi_\alpha^{\dagger} = \Pi_\alpha$, and $\sum_\alpha \Pi_\alpha = \mathbb{1}$
inside the sector.

\subsection{Orbits, and why the projector is cheap}

The action of $\Gk$ on the $M_{\mathbf{n}}$ columns of the sector partitions them
into orbits. Because $\Pi_\alpha$ commutes with every $U(R)$, it does not mix
orbits: $\Pi_\alpha$ maps the span of an orbit onto itself. This observation is
what makes the projection practical, and it is worth spelling out.

Restricted to one orbit of size $o$, the operator \eqref{eq:Pi} is an $o\times o$
Hermitian matrix that can be written down from the phases \eqref{eq:phased}
alone. Its eigenvectors with eigenvalue $1$ are an orthonormal basis of the
$\alpha$-isotypic component of that orbit's span; its other eigenvalues vanish.
Diagonalizing $o\times o$ matrices and collecting the eigenvectors with
eigenvalue $1$ therefore produces an orthonormal basis $V$ of the whole
$\alpha$-isotypic component, because orbits are disjoint and vectors supported on
different orbits are orthogonal. The cost is
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{\text{orbits}}\Bigl(|\Gk|\,o^{2} + o^{3}\Bigr), \qquad o \le |\Gk| \le |\Gpt| ,
\end{equation}
and it does not grow with the sector dimension $M_{\mathbf{n}}$: the largest
matrix that is ever diagonalized has the size of the little group, twelve for
$D_6$.

The alternative, projecting each basis vector and then orthonormalizing the
result, is both more expensive and more delicate: for $d_\alpha > 1$ the
projected vectors are neither orthogonal nor independent, and the Gram matrix
that repairs them is of the size of the sector. The orbit decomposition avoids
that matrix entirely. In floating point the eigenvalue test needs a threshold;
eigenvalues of $\Pi_\alpha$ are $0$ or $1$, so a cut at $1/2$ is safe.

\subsection{The block}

With $V$ the orthonormal basis of the $\alpha$-isotypic component, the block is
\begin{equation}
  H_{\mathbf{n},\alpha} = V^{\dagger}H_{\mathbf{n}}V .
  \label{eq:block}
\end{equation}
Since $[H,U(R)] = 0$ and $\Pi_\alpha$ is a projector onto an invariant subspace,
$H_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}$ is the restriction of $H$ to that subspace, and the
eigenvalues of $H$ are the union over $(\mathbf{n},\alpha)$ of the eigenvalues of
the blocks. Completeness can be checked without diagonalizing anything:
$\sum_\alpha \dim H_{\mathbf{n},\alpha} = M_{\mathbf{n}}$ for every momentum and
$\sum_{\mathbf{n}} M_{\mathbf{n}} = \dim\Hil$.

\section{The spin flip}\label{sec:spin}

For a spin model there is one further symmetry that has nothing to do with the
lattice. The spin flip sends every local state to its mirror image,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{F}\ket{\sigma_1\dots\sigma_L} = \ket{\bar\sigma_1\dots\bar\sigma_L},
  \qquad \bar\sigma = d_{\mathrm{loc}}-1-\sigma ,
  \label{eq:flip}
\end{equation}
and it commutes with every space-group element, since it acts on the local state
at each site and the space group only permutes sites. Hence
$G = \Glatt\times\mathbb{Z}_2$, and the irreducible representations are the
products $(\mathbf{n},\alpha)\otimes s$ with $s = \pm1$ the parity.

The charge is not preserved by $\mathcal{F}$: it replaces every local charge by
$\bar q(\sigma) = q(d_{\mathrm{loc}}-1-\sigma)$, so a state of charge $Q$ is sent
to one of charge
\begin{equation}
  Q' = \sum_j \bar q(\sigma_j) .
\end{equation}
For a spin model, where $q(\sigma) = \sigma$ and hence
$\bar q(\sigma) = d_{\mathrm{loc}}-1-\sigma$, this is
$Q' = L(d_{\mathrm{loc}}-1) - Q$. The spin flip is a symmetry of a fixed charge
sector only when that sector is mapped onto itself, $Q' = Q$; for the spin model
this reads
\begin{equation}
  2Q = L(d_{\mathrm{loc}}-1).
  \label{eq:selfconj}
\end{equation}
and outside that case asking for it is an error rather than a symmetry. (In the
full space it is a symmetry, and it relates the sectors $Q$ and $Q'$.)

Inside a sector, $\mathcal{F}$ is a permutation of the computational basis
without phases, so it acts on the adapted basis as a phased permutation like any
other element, and its square is the identity. The parity projector is
\begin{equation}
  \Pi_{s} = \tfrac12\bigl(\mathbb{1} + s\,\mathcal{F}\bigr),
\end{equation}
and it acts inside each $(\mathbf{n},\alpha)$ block, because $\mathcal{F}$
commutes with $H$ and with every $U(R)$. Equivalently one uses the character
$\chi_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}(\cdot)\chi_s(\cdot)$ of the product group and projects
once in the enlarged group; the two give the same blocks. Whether a given block
splits at all is decided by the spectrum: levels with $s = +1$ and $s=-1$ that
coincide are not resolved by the parity, they are degenerate.

\section{The algorithm and its cost}\label{sec:algorithm}

The construction of the preceding sections is summarized below. The data
structures matter for the cost and are named.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Basis.} Enumerate the computational basis of the charge sector by a
depth-first walk over the sites that prunes on the remaining charge, and store
each state as a packed integer with one field per site. Keep the map from a state
to its index: a hash table, or a direct table when the code space is small enough.
The cost is the dimension of the sector, up to a factor $d_{\mathrm{loc}}$.

\item \textbf{Actions.} For every element of the group store the site permutation
and, per site, the local permutation and phase of \eqref{eq:action}. Applying an
element to a state is then a loop over the sites, with no search.

\item \textbf{Hamiltonian.} Assemble $H$ in the computational basis from the terms
\eqref{eq:hamiltonian}: for each state, apply each term and accumulate the image
and its coefficient, then sort and merge equal images. The cost is proportional
to the number of nonzeros of $H$: at most the dimension, times the number of
terms, times the largest number of images one term produces, and in practice far
less than that.

\item \textbf{Symmetrisation} (optional, and unnecessary when the couplings
already respect the group). If the Hamiltonian is given as a single generating
term, or as a set of terms that is not closed under the group, replace it by
\begin{equation}
  H \mapsto \frac{1}{|\Glatt|}\sum_{g\in\Glatt} U(g)\,H\,U(g)^{-1}.
  \label{eq:sym}
\end{equation}
This is the projector onto the operators that commute with the group. On an
invariant input it is the identity, so it may be applied without changing the
model; on a non-invariant one it silently replaces the model by its average. One
sparse comparison with the input separates the two cases, and it is worth making,
because the second one is otherwise invisible. Note that the prefactor is $1/|\Glatt|$, not a normalization chosen to
suit the model: symmetrising a single term and then forgetting the factor scales
the Hamiltonian, and every energy with it.

\item \textbf{Momentum sectors.} For every $\mathbf{n}$, walk the translation
orbits once: for each representative apply the translations, accumulate the
column \eqref{eq:adapt} with its phases, normalize, and discard it if the norm
\eqref{eq:norm} vanishes. Per momentum the cost is $|\Gtr|$ state actions per
orbit, that is $|\Gtr|$ times the dimension of the charge sector, so $|\Gtr|^{2}$
times the dimension over the whole Brillouin zone.

\item \textbf{Sector Hamiltonians.} Form $H_{\mathbf{n}} =
B_{\mathbf{n}}^{\dagger}HB_{\mathbf{n}}$ as a sparse product, column by column;
never form the dense $B_{\mathbf{n}}$. The work is the number of nonzeros of the
intermediate $HB_{\mathbf{n}}$, that is the length of a column of
$B_{\mathbf{n}}$ times the number of rows it reaches, summed over the columns;
in practice the columns are short and this step is far cheaper than the
assembly of $H$ itself.

\item \textbf{Little group and phases.} For each momentum, select the operations
that satisfy \eqref{eq:little} and compute their phased permutation
\eqref{eq:phased} on the sector columns. If an operation in the little group
sends a column outside the sector, the construction has a defect and this is
where it shows: the code raises rather than continuing.

\item \textbf{Blocks.} Group the columns into orbits of $\Gk$, diagonalize the
projector \eqref{eq:Pi} orbit by orbit, keep the eigenvectors with unit
eigenvalue, and form $V^{\dagger}H_{\mathbf{n}}V$.

\item \textbf{Diagonalize.} With the blocks in hand, use a dense solver for the
small ones and an iterative one for the large ones. The two are not
interchangeable: a single-vector Lanczos recursion resolves distinct levels only,
so a degenerate multiplet contributes one Ritz value and not one per copy, and an
unconverged Ritz value is a number between two levels and not an eigenvalue of
anything. Either compare against distinct levels or read multiplicities from the
character of $H$ itself, and check residuals before accepting a Ritz value.
\end{enumerate}

The cost of the symmetry machinery is dominated by step~5, which is
$|\Gtr|$ state actions per orbit, and by step~7, whose largest matrix has the
size of the little group. Neither grows with the block dimension. What grows is
step~9: the eigensolver is the only part of the procedure that scales as the cube
of the block, and it dominates for any lattice on which the method is worth
using.

\section{Numerical checks}\label{sec:checks}

The construction was checked against direct diagonalization; a sector
decomposition that is wrong in any of the ways listed in
Section~\ref{sec:pitfalls} fails at least one of the following.

\paragraph{Completeness.} $\sum_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}\dim H_{\mathbf{n},\alpha}$
equals the dimension of the Hilbert space, and $\sum_{\mathbf{n}}M_{\mathbf{n}}$
equals it too. This catches a wrong little group immediately, since a group that
is too large produces projections onto non-invariant subspaces.

\paragraph{Spectra.} The union of the block spectra equals the spectrum of $H$
obtained by diagonalizing it directly, level by level:
\begin{equation}
  \max\bigl|\,\operatorname{spec}(H)_{\text{blocks}} -
  \operatorname{spec}(H)_{\text{direct}}\,\bigr| \lesssim 10^{-13}
\end{equation}
for the $3\times3$ models with $D_4$ and with $D_6$, in the full Hilbert space.
For the four-by-four square lattice at half filling ($12\,870$ states, $D_4$) the
ground-state energy obtained from the blocks,
$E_0 = -44.9139328337$ in units where the Heisenberg coupling is written with
Pauli matrices, agrees with a Lanczos diagonalization of the same Hamiltonian to
all ten printed digits.

\paragraph{Labels.} Each adapted vector carries a momentum, an irrep label and a
parity; applying the corresponding operators to it returns the same vector with
the expected phase, to $10^{-15}$. This is the check that catches a missing Bloch
phase, and the check that the spin-flip parity is resolved rather than merely
computed.

\paragraph{Group law and character tables.} The site maps compose as the group
does,
\begin{equation}
  U(g)U(h) = U(gh),
\end{equation}
and the character tables of all little groups that occur are orthonormal and
satisfy $\sum_\alpha d_\alpha^2 = |\Gk|$. For a generic finite group, whose
multiplication table is what one actually knows, the irreducible characters are
computed from the class algebra: the matrices
$(N_\mu)_{\nu\lambda} = a^{\lambda}_{\mu\nu}$ of structure constants commute and
are diagonalized by a common basis, whose eigenvalues are the central characters
$\omega_\rho(\mu)$; then
\begin{equation}
  d_\rho^2 = \frac{|G|}{\sum_\mu |\omega_\rho(\mu)|^2/|C_\mu|},
  \qquad
  \chi_\rho(\mu) = \frac{d_\rho\,\omega_\rho(\mu)}{|C_\mu|}.
  \label{eq:chars}
\end{equation}

\section{What goes wrong}\label{sec:pitfalls}

The construction has several failure modes that are silent, in the sense that
the program runs and produces numbers. They are collected here because each of
them was, at some point, observed in the implementation.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{A little group that is the whole point group.} The criterion
\eqref{eq:little} must be evaluated modulo $N_i$ on the integer label. Testing
instead that $R\mathbf{k}$ equals $\mathbf{k}$ up to a reciprocal lattice vector
accepts everything, because on a finite torus all allowed momenta lie in the same
coset. The symptom is a decomposition with far too few sectors and labels that do
not reproduce under the operators.

\item \emph{A missing Bloch phase.} Inside a sector the operations act by phased
permutations \eqref{eq:phased}. Replacing the phases by one makes the blocks
Hermitian and plausible, and shifts the spectrum by an amount of order the
bandwidth, $1.8$ on the $3\times3$ square lattice with $D_4$.

\item \emph{A model that the group does not actually leave invariant.} The
symmetrisation \eqref{eq:sym} repairs an asymmetric input by averaging it, which
makes the output invariant but changes the model. A bond list that looks
periodic may not be: an ordering guard such as ``keep the bond if the first site
has the smaller index'' drops exactly the bonds that close the torus, and the raw
Hamiltonian is then neither translation nor point-group invariant. Symmetrising
it produces the average of an open lattice, which is a different model.
Comparing \eqref{eq:sym} with its input costs one sparse subtraction and detects
this at once.

\item \emph{A non-monomial local action.} If the local representation is not
monomial in the chosen basis, the image of a basis state is a superposition and
the adapted basis is not what the construction assumes. The cure is a change of
local basis, not a change of algorithm.

\item \emph{A forced spin flip.} The spin flip is a symmetry of a charge sector
only under \eqref{eq:selfconj}. Applying it outside that case produces blocks
that are not the restriction of $H$ to anything.

\item \emph{Lanczos without a residual test.} See step~9 above. An unconverged
Ritz value is not an eigenvalue, and a converged recursion still reports one
vector per distinct level, not one per copy.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Concluding remarks}

The decomposition described here is the standard treatment of space-group
symmetry in a crystalline solid, applied to a finite torus and to the compressed
basis that a computer uses. Two features are worth keeping in view when adapting
it. The first is that the two-step structure is not an approximation: translations
give an abelian normal subgroup, whose one-dimensional representations are
labelled by an integer vector, and the little group is what remains of the point
group at that label; the induced representations are the bookkeeping that
restores the whole star. The second is that the cost of the symmetry machinery is
independent of the size of the blocks: it is set by the number of states, the
number of translations, and the order of the little group. Once the decomposition
is correct, the remaining bottleneck is the diagonalization itself, and that is
the point at which one should reach for iterative methods, distributed memory, or
a matrix-free formulation of the block.
